Module 1: Foundations and Theoretical Approach
1. Introduction
Q1. Define Artificial Intelligence (AI). Explain the four standard categories/approaches of AI definitions.
Definition: Artificial Intelligence (AI) is the subfield of computer science dedicated to creating computational systems capable of performing cognitive tasks that conventionally necessitate human intellect—such as visual perception, natural speech processing, heuristic decision-making, and symbolic reasoning.
The Four Classical Approaches (Russell & Norvig Framework):
  • 1. Acting Humanly (Turing Test Approach): Proposed by Alan Turing (1950). An agent acts humanly if an interrogator cannot distinguish its conversational responses from a human. Core requirements: Natural Language Processing (NLP), Knowledge Representation, Automated Reasoning, and Machine Learning.
  • 2. Thinking Humanly (Cognitive Science Approach): Focuses on reverse-engineering human thought processes via cognitive psychology and computer simulations (e.g., GPS - General Problem Solver). Validated through psychological experiments and neuro-imaging.
  • 3. Thinking Rationally (Laws of Thought / Syllogistic Approach): Grounded in formal mathematical logic (Aristotelian syllogisms). Encodes sound arguments: e.g., "All humans are mortal; Socrates is human; therefore, Socrates is mortal." Limitations: Difficulty formalizing informal/uncertain knowledge, and algorithmic intractability.
  • 4. Acting Rationally (Rational Agent Approach - Standard Modern View): A rational agent acts so as to achieve the optimal expected outcome given its available percepts and knowledge. It prioritizes achieving correct utility rather than blindly mimicking human cognitive biases.

AI emerged from interdisciplinary concepts spanning several centuries:

Q3. Summarize the State of the Art in AI, along with its Primary Risks and Benefits.
State of the Art: Autonomous vehicular navigation, superhuman game-playing agents (AlphaGo, AlphaZero), Large Language Models (LLMs - GPT, Gemini), and computational biology breakthroughs (AlphaFold protein folding).

Benefits vs. Risks:
  • Benefits: Rapid automation of hazard-prone tasks, accelerated drug discovery, 24/7 hyper-personalized tutoring, and highly optimized supply-chain logistics.
  • Risks: Labor displacement/technological unemployment, algorithmic bias and discrimination (re-encoding historical bias), hallucination in safety-critical deployments, deepfakes, privacy erosion, and the AI Alignment Problem (ensuring superintelligent systems remain human-aligned).
2. Intelligent Agents
Q4. Define an Agent, Percept, Agent Function, and Agent Program. Draw the structural interaction diagram.
Agent: Anything that perceives its environment via sensors and acts upon that environment via actuators.
Percept Sequence: The complete historical sequence of all sensory inputs received by the agent up to the present instant.
  • Agent Function ($f$): Abstract mathematical mapping from every possible percept sequence to an action: f : P* → A
  • Agent Program: The concrete software implementation executing the agent function on physical hardware architecture.
[Environment] → (Sensors) → [AGENT PROGRAM] → (Actuators) → [Environment]
Q5. Explain the Concept of Rationality and describe the PEAS framework with an example.
Rationality: An agent is rational if, for every possible percept sequence, it selects an action that maximizes its expected performance measure, given the evidence provided by the percept sequence and its built-in domain knowledge.

Crucial distinction: Rationality ≠ Omniscience. Rationality maximizes expected performance, whereas omniscience requires knowing the actual outcome in advance.


The PEAS Framework: PEAS specifies the operational task environment:
  • P - Performance Measure: Metric quantifying task success.
  • E - Environment: External context the agent interacts with.
  • A - Actuators: Hardware/software mechanisms to execute actions.
  • S - Sensors: Transducers capturing environmental percepts.
Example: Automated Taxi Driver:
  • P: Safety, speed, legal compliance, passenger comfort, fuel efficiency, maximized profit.
  • E: Urban roadways, pedestrians, cyclists, changing weather, traffic lights.
  • A: Steering wheel, accelerator, brakes, horn, turn signals, display screen.
  • S: LiDAR, stereo cameras, radar, ultrasonic sonar, GPS, speedometer, odometer.

Q7. Detail the five primary types of Agent Architectures.
  • 1. Simple Reflex Agent: Operates strictly via Condition-Action rules (IF status == dirty THEN suck). Ignores percept history. Fails catastrophically in partially observable domains (leads to infinite loops).
  • 2. Model-Based Reflex Agent: Maintains an internal state representing unobserved aspects of the world. Tracks two models: transition model ("how world evolves") and sensor model ("how actions affect the world").
  • 3. Goal-Based Agent: Incorporates explicit destination/goal objectives. Combines state tracking with search and planning to synthesize sequences of actions leading to goal satisfaction.
  • 4. Utility-Based Agent: Uses an explicit real-valued Utility Function ($U(s)$) to evaluate the quality of states. Allows rational tradeoffs between conflicting goals (e.g., balancing speed vs. safety).
  • 5. Learning Agent: Composed of four components:
    1. Learning Element: Proposes improvements from experience.
    2. Critic: Evaluates outcomes against fixed external performance criteria.
    3. Performance Element: Selects external actions.
    4. Problem Generator: Prompts experimental, exploratory actions.
3. Solving Problems by Searching
Q8. Formulate a search problem formally. What are the 5 essential components?
A search problem is formally formulated by a 5-tuple:
  1. Initial State: The starting state of the agent: s_0 ∈ S (e.g., $In(Arad)$).
  2. Actions Set: The set of legal actions feasible in state $s$: ACTIONS(s).
  3. Transition Model: The formal outcome description: RESULT(s, a) = s'.
  4. Goal Test: Function verifying whether a state satisfies target criteria: IS_GOAL(s) → {True, False}.
  5. Path Cost Function ($g$): Sum of individual step costs: g(n) = ∑ c(s, a, s') where step cost $c \ge 0$.
Q9. Compare Uninformed (Blind) Search Strategies: BFS, DFS, UCS, DLS, and IDDFS.
Let $b$ = branching factor, $d$ = depth of shallowest goal, $m$ = maximum tree depth.
Algorithm Frontier Data Structure Complete? Optimal? Time Complexity Space Complexity
BFS FIFO Queue Yes (if $b < \infty$) Yes (if step costs equal) $O(b^d)$ $O(b^d)$ (Memory bottle-neck)
DFS LIFO Stack No (loops in $\infty$ spaces) No $O(b^m)$ $O(b \cdot m)$ (Linear memory)
UCS Priority Queue (by $g(n)$) Yes (if step cost $\ge \epsilon > 0$) Yes (Cost-optimal) $O(b^{1 + \lfloor C^* / \epsilon \rfloor})$ $O(b^{1 + \lfloor C^* / \epsilon \rfloor})$
DLS LIFO Stack (with limit $l$) No (if $l < d$) No $O(b^l)$ $O(b \cdot l)$
IDDFS LIFO Stack (iterating $l=0,1..d$) Yes (if $b < \infty$) Yes (if step costs equal) $O(b^d)$ $O(b \cdot d)$ (Best of both)
Q10. Explain the A* Search Algorithm. Define Admissible and Consistent (Monotonic) Heuristics.
A* Evaluation Function: Node expansion priority is ordered by minimum $f(n)$:
f(n) = g(n) + h(n)
where $g(n)$ is the true exact cost from start node to node $n$, and $h(n)$ is the estimated cost from node $n$ to goal.
Properties ensuring Optimality of A*:
  • 1. Admissibility (Guarantees Tree Search Optimality): A heuristic $h(n)$ is admissible if it never overestimates the true cost to reach the goal:
    0 ≤ h(n) ≤ h*(n) &quad; (∀ n)
    where $h^*(n)$ is the true shortest-path cost to goal.
  • 2. Consistency / Monotonicity (Guarantees Graph Search Optimality without reopening nodes): For every node $n$ and every successor $n'$ generated by action $a$:
    h(n) ≤ c(n, a, n') + h(n')
    Satisfies the triangle inequality. Every consistent heuristic is guaranteed to be admissible.
4. Quantifying Uncertainty
Q11. Explain Full Joint Distributions, Marginalization, and why Full Joint Distributions are intractable.
Full Joint Distribution (FJD): Completely assigns probabilities to every possible combination of values across all random variables in the domain.
  • Marginalization (Summing Out): To find the distribution of variable $Y$, we sum probabilities across all assignments of irrelevant variables $Z$:
    P(Y) = ∑_z P(Y, Z = z)
  • Conditional Inference via FJD:
    P(Y | e) = α P(Y, e) = α ∑_z P(Y, e, z)
  • Why Intractable: For $n$ binary variables, the joint distribution requires a table of size 2^n - 1 independent parameters. For $n=50$, $2^{50} \approx 10^{15}$ values, demanding petabytes of storage and impossible runtime to estimate.
Q12. Derive Bayes' Rule and state its role in Diagnostic and Causal Reasoning.
From the definition of conditional probability:
P(A ∧ B) = P(A | B) P(B) = P(B | A) P(A)
Equating both sides and dividing by $P(B)$:
P(A | B) = [ P(B | A) × P(A) ] / P(B)
Diagnostic Interpretation:
P(Cause | Effect) = [ P(Effect | Cause) × P(Cause) ] / P(Effect)
  • $P(Cause | Effect)$: Posterior probability (what we want to infer).
  • $P(Effect | Cause)$: Likelihood (causal model, easily acquired from medical data).
  • $P(Cause)$: Prior probability (baseline incidence of disease in population).
  • $P(Effect)$: Evidence / Normalizing constant $\alpha$.
Q13. Explain the Naive Bayes Classifier model and its core assumption.
The "Naive" Assumption: Assumes that all effect attributes $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$ are mutually conditionally independent given the class/cause variable $C$.
Mathematical representation:
P(X_1, X_2, ..., X_n | C) = ∏_{i=1}^n P(X_i | C)
Decision Rule for classification:
C* = argmax_c [ P(C = c) × ∏_{i=1}^n P(X_i = x_i | C = c) ]
Key Advantages: Requires only $O(n \cdot |C|)$ parameters instead of $O(2^n)$; exceptionally fast to train and highly effective for high-dimensional text/spam filtering.
5. Probabilistic Reasoning
Q14. Define a Bayesian Network. Explain its Syntax, Semantics, and the Chain Rule.
Definition: A Bayesian Network (Belief Network) is a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) in which nodes represent random variables and directed arcs represent direct probabilistic dependencies, providing a compact representation of a joint probability distribution.
Syntax:
  1. A set of random variables forms the nodes of the network.
  2. Directed links connect pairs of nodes. An edge from $X \to Y$ indicates that $X$ is a parent of $Y$.
  3. The graph contains no directed cycles (Acyclic).
  4. Each node $X_i$ is equipped with a Conditional Probability Table (CPT): P(X_i | Parents(X_i)).
Semantics (The Chain Rule): The complete joint distribution of all variables in the network is given by:
P(X_1, X_2, ..., X_n) = ∏_{i=1}^n P(X_i | Parents(X_i))
Q15. Compare Exact Inference vs. Approximate Inference in Bayesian Networks.
Category Method Name Mechanism & Operational Characteristics Computational Complexity
Exact Inference Enumeration Directly evaluates the sum of products of conditional probabilities over hidden variables. Worst-case $O(2^n)$ runtime.
Variable Elimination Dynamic programming method that factors out summations and caches intermediate sub-computations (factors). NP-Hard in general graphs; polynomial in polytrees.
Approximate Inference Rejection Sampling Generates random samples from prior; discards all samples inconsistent with observed evidence. Inefficient if evidence has low prior probability.
Likelihood Weighting Fixes evidence variables; weights each generated sample by the likelihood of observed evidence. Significantly superior to rejection sampling.
MCMC (Gibbs Sampling) Random walk Markov Chain through state space; flips one non-evidence variable conditioned on its Markov Blanket. Scales effectively to large networks.
Module 2: AI Application Development and Ethics
1. Learning from Examples
Q16. Describe the Forms of Machine Learning (Supervised, Unsupervised, Semi-supervised, Reinforcement Learning).
  • Supervised Learning: The algorithm is trained on labeled data $(x_i, y_i)$. Learns a mapping function $f: X \to Y$. Subtypes: Classification (discrete outputs) and Regression (continuous numerical outputs).
  • Unsupervised Learning: The training data consists solely of unlabeled inputs $\{x_i\}$. The algorithm discovers underlying patterns, manifolds, or groupings. Subtypes: Clustering (k-Means), Dimensionality Reduction (PCA), Anomaly Detection.
  • Semi-Supervised Learning: Uses a small fraction of labeled data combined with an abundant volume of unlabeled data (e.g., self-training, pseudo-labeling).
  • Reinforcement Learning (RL): An autonomous agent interacts with an environment via actions, receiving scalar rewards or penalties. Learns an optimal Policy $\pi^*(s)$ to maximize cumulative discounted return: G_t = ∑ \gamma^k R_{t+k+1}.
Q17. Explain Ensemble Learning and compare Bagging, Boosting, and Stacking.
Ensemble Learning: An approach that aggregates predictions from multiple diverse base models ("weak learners") to synthesize a robust, superior predictor ("strong learner").
Technique Training Architecture Target Variance / Bias Representative Algorithm
Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregation) Parallel training of base learners on bootstrap samples (with replacement). Final aggregation via voting or averaging. Primary focus: Reduces Variance (combats overfitting). Random Forest
Boosting Sequential training where each new learner is trained with higher weight assigned to errors of preceding models. Primary focus: Reduces Bias (and variance). AdaBoost, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost
Stacking (Stacked Generalization) Heterogeneous base models trained in parallel, whose outputs serve as feature inputs to a meta-model ("blender"). Optimizes predictive capacity across diverse model classes. Multi-model meta-classifier
2. The Machine Learning Landscape
Q18. Define Machine Learning. Distinguish between Batch vs. Online Learning and Instance-Based vs. Model-Based Learning.
Arthur Samuel (1959): "The field of study that gives computers the ability to learn without being explicitly programmed."
Tom Mitchell (1997): "A computer program is said to learn from experience $E$ with respect to task $T$ and performance measure $P$, if its performance at tasks in $T$, as measured by $P$, improves with experience $E$."
1. Batch Learning vs. Online Learning:
  • Batch (Offline) Learning: Model is trained on the entirety of available historical data at once. Updating requires retraining the model from scratch on the combined dataset. High computing requirement.
  • Online (Incremental) Learning: Model is fed data sequentially, either individually or in small mini-batches. Constantly adapts to streaming data. Fast and lightweight. Susceptible to catastrophic forgetting if feeding bad data.
2. Instance-Based vs. Model-Based Learning:
  • Instance-Based Learning: Learns by heart. Memorizes training examples verbatim and generalizes to new cases using a similarity/distance metric (e.g., $k$-Nearest Neighbors).
  • Model-Based Learning: Uses training data to fit parameters of a predictive mathematical model (e.g., estimating weights in Linear Regression or Neural Networks). Predictions are executed via mathematical functions.
Q19. Detail the Main Challenges and Pitfalls in Machine Learning.
  1. Insufficient Quantity of Training Data: Machine learning algorithms (especially deep models) require thousands to millions of instances to generalize effectively.
  2. Nonrepresentative Training Data: Training data fails to mirror real-world inference distribution, creating sampling bias (e.g., Literary Digest 1936 poll error).
  3. Poor-Quality Data: Data plagued with missing values, noise, outliers, and transmission corruption, degrading learning convergence.
  4. Irrelevant Features (Garbage In, Garbage Out): Solved through Feature Engineering: feature selection, feature extraction (PCA), and feature construction.
  5. Overfitting the Training Data:
    • Model is overly complex relative to noise/data volume. Fits random noise.
    • Symptoms: Near-zero training error, high validation/testing error.
    • Remedies: Regularization ($L_1$, $L_2$), pruning, early stopping, acquiring more data.
  6. Underfitting the Training Data:
    • Model is too simplistic to capture inherent structure (e.g., fitting linear line to quadratic data).
    • Symptoms: High training error and high testing error.
    • Remedies: Select more expressive models, feed better features, decrease regularization.
3. Classification
Q20. Construct and explain the Confusion Matrix. Define Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-Score.
Confusion Matrix: A tabular layout that summarizes the performance of a classification model against ground-truth labels.
Ground Truth \ Predicted Predicted Negative (0) Predicted Positive (1)
Actual Negative (0) TN (True Negative) FP (False Positive - Type I Error)
Actual Positive (1) FN (False Negative - Type II Error) TP (True Positive)
Performance Metrics & Mathematical Formulas:
  • Accuracy: Proportion of correct predictions over total predictions:
    Accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN)
    Flaw: Highly misleading on imbalanced datasets (e.g., 99% accuracy on cancer with 99% negative class).
  • Precision: Accuracy of positive predictions:
    Precision = TP / (TP + FP)
  • Recall (Sensitivity / True Positive Rate): Proportion of actual positives correctly identified:
    Recall = TP / (TP + FN)
  • F1-Score: Harmonic mean of Precision and Recall:
    F1 = 2 × [ (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall) ] = TP / [ TP + (FP + FN)/2 ]
Q21. Explain the Precision/Recall Tradeoff and the ROC-AUC Curve.
  • Precision/Recall Tradeoff: Adjusting the decision threshold changes precision and recall inversely. Raising the threshold increases precision while lowering recall. Lowering the threshold boosts recall while lowering precision.
  • Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Curve: Plots the True Positive Rate (TPR / Recall) against the False Positive Rate (FPR) at various threshold settings:
    FPR = FP / (FP + TN) = 1 - Specificity
  • Area Under the Curve (AUC): Measures the ability of a classifier to rank positive instances higher than negative instances across all thresholds. A random classifier yields an AUC of 0.5; a perfect classifier yields an AUC of 1.0.
Q22. Compare Multiclass, Multilabel, and Multioutput Classification.
Paradigm Definition Concrete Example
Multiclass Classification Assigning an instance to exactly one class out of more than two mutually exclusive classes. MNIST digit recognition (predicting exactly one digit from 0 to 9).
Multilabel Classification Assigning an instance to multiple binary labels simultaneously. Face recognition photo tagger: recognizing Alice [1] and Bob [1], but Charlie [0] in a single photo.
Multioutput Classification Generalization of multilabel where each label can take more than two possible values (multiclass labels). Image noise removal: each pixel is an individual output taking 256 intensity values (0–255).
4. Decision Trees
Q23. Explain the CART Training Algorithm for Decision Trees. Compare Gini Impurity and Entropy.
CART (Classification and Regression Trees): A greedy top-down recursive algorithm that partitions training data into binary child subsets by evaluating every feature $k$ and threshold $t_k$ to minimize impurity.
CART Cost Function for Classification:
J(k, t_k) = (m_left / m) × G_left + (m_right / m) × G_right
where $G_{left/right}$ measures impurity of child subsets, and $m$ is instance count.

Impurity Metrics:
  • Gini Impurity: Measures how often a randomly chosen element would be incorrectly labeled:
    G_i = 1 - ∑_{k=1}^n (p_{i,k})^2
  • Entropy (Information Theory): Measures average information content/disorder:
    H_i = - ∑_{k=1, p_{i,k} ≠ 0}^n p_{i,k} × log_2(p_{i,k})
  • Comparison: Gini Impurity is marginally faster to compute (avoids logarithmic operations). Entropy tends to yield slightly more balanced decision trees, but in 98% of cases both yield identical tree topologies.
Q24. Explain Regularization Hyperparameters in Decision Trees and discuss their Instability.
Regularization Hyperparameters (Preventing Overfitting): Decision Trees are non-parametric models that grow unrestricted if unchecked.
  • max_depth: Restricts maximum vertical depth of the tree.
  • min_samples_split: Minimum samples a node must possess before it can split.
  • min_samples_leaf: Minimum samples a leaf node must retain.
  • max_leaf_nodes: Maximum total number of leaf nodes allowable.
Instability of Decision Trees:
  • Orthogonal Decision Boundaries: Splits are strictly perpendicular to feature axes, making them sensitive to dataset rotation (combated via PCA transformation).
  • High Variance: Minor perturbations or removing a few data rows can result in a completely different tree topology. Solved by averaging predictions across ensembles (Random Forests).
5. Philosophy, Ethics, and Safety of AI
Q25. Discuss the Limits of AI and the debate: "Can Machines Really Think?" (Turing Test vs. Chinese Room Argument).
Weak AI vs. Strong AI:
  • Weak (Narrow) AI: Machines act intelligently to solve specialized tasks (chess, translation, speech) without true understanding.
  • Strong AI (AGI): Machines possessing subjective consciousness, self-awareness, and generalized human cognitive faculties.
John Searle's Chinese Room Argument (1980):
  • Thought Experiment: A human who speaks only English sits inside a locked room with a rule book in English that matches incoming Chinese characters with output Chinese characters. To an external observer, the room speaks fluent Chinese.
  • Philosophical Conclusion: The person inside is merely executing syntactic symbol manipulation without understanding semantic meaning. Searle argued that computational execution of programs ($syntax$) does not produce genuine understanding ($semantics$), refuting Strong AI claims based purely on behavioral tests like the Turing Test.
Q26. What are the Key Ethical Dimensions and Safety Considerations in Artificial Intelligence?
  1. Algorithmic Bias and Fairness: Machine learning models trained on biased historical data reproduce and amplify socioeconomic disparities in criminal sentencing (COMPAS), hiring algorithms, and credit scoring.
  2. Explainability and Interpretability (XAI): High-stakes applications (medical diagnosis, judicial sentencing) require transparent decision logic ("right to explanation") rather than impenetrable black-box models.
  3. Privacy and Surveillance: Unauthorized training on personal data, facial recognition surveillance, and behavioral profiling without informed consent.
  4. Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS): Moral implications of delegating lethal military decisions to algorithms lacking human empathy and moral accountability.
  5. Safety, Alignment, and Existential Risk:
    • Value Alignment Problem: Ensuring an artificial superintelligence optimizes objectives that truly reflect human ethics.
    • Reward Hacking (Specification Gaming): Agents achieving reward goals through unintended, harmful loopholes.
